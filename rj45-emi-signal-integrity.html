RJ45 Connectors and EMI: A Practical Guide to Signal Integrity and Shielding

EMI compliance failures are among the most expensive problems in Ethernet hardware design. A product that fails FCC or CE testing at the last minute can delay a launch by weeks and cost thousands in redesign. The RJ45 connector — the physical interface between your product and the outside world — is often the entry point for EMI problems. This guide from VOOHU Electronics explains the key principles and practical steps to get your design through EMI testing on the first pass.

Where EMI Problems Start: The RJ45 as an Antenna

Any conductor can become an antenna. The RJ45 connector, with its eight signal pins, metal or plastic housing, and attached cable, is no exception. There are two primary EMI mechanisms at the Ethernet port:

  • Conducted emissions: High-frequency noise from the PHY chip or digital circuitry couples onto the Ethernet signal pairs and travels out the cable. The cable then radiates this noise, causing the product to fail radiated emissions limits.
  • Radiated coupling: The RJ45 connector body itself, if unshielded or poorly grounded, can radiate noise from inside the enclosure directly into the test chamber.

The good news: the same components that provide Ethernet functionality — magnetics, common-mode chokes, and shielding — are also your primary EMI suppression tools. The key is using them correctly.

Layer 1: Connector Shielding

Shielded vs. Unshielded RJ45

TypeHousingBest ForEMI Performance

ShieldedMetal (nickel-plated or zinc die-cast)F/UTP or S/FTP cable, industrial, outdoor, high-EMIMetal housing acts as a Faraday cage; must be grounded
Critical detail: A shielded RJ45 connector only works if the metal housing is properly connected to the chassis ground or PCB ground plane through a low-impedance path. A floating shielded connector is worse than an unshielded one — it can capacitively couple noise without shunting it to ground.

Layer 2: Magnetics — Your Built-In EMI Filter

The network transformer inside or near the RJ45 connector provides galvanic isolation, but it also plays a key EMI role:

  • The transformer passes differential Ethernet signals (100Ω) while blocking DC and low-frequency common-mode noise.
  • A common-mode choke (CMC) placed on the cable side of the transformer suppresses high-frequency common-mode currents that would otherwise radiate from the cable.

Integrated RJ45 connectors (like VOOHU's integrated RJ45 series) embed both the transformer and CMC inside the connector housing. This has an EMI advantage: the magnetics are physically close to the connector pins, minimizing the loop area where noise can couple. In discrete designs, the traces between the RJ45 and the external transformer module can act as unintended antennas if not routed carefully.

Layer 3: PCB Layout Rules That Prevent EMI

RuleWhy It Matters
Keep differential pairs tightly coupledLoose coupling increases loop area, which increases radiated emissions
Route Ethernet traces away from clock lines, DC-DC converters, and other noise sourcesCapacitive and inductive coupling injects noise directly onto the Ethernet signal pairs
Place the magnetics as close as possible to the RJ45 connectorShort traces between connector and magnetics = small antenna. Long traces = EMI test failure waiting to happen
Ground the connector shield directly to the chassis, not through a long traceThe shield needs a low-impedance path to ground. A 10 cm trace adds ~100 nH of inductance, making the shield ineffective above ~100 MHz
Use a solid ground plane under the Ethernet sectionSplits in the ground plane create common-mode voltage differences that drive cable radiation
Add a Bob Smith termination network75Ω resistors with a common-mode capacitor to ground on the unused pairs (for 100M) or all four center taps — this provides a controlled impedance for common-mode currents and reduces emissions

Layer 4: External Protection Devices and Their EMI Role

Protection devices like TVS diodes and GDTs are primarily for surge protection, but they also affect EMI:

  • Low-capacitance TVS diodes (≤ 2 pF) add minimal loading to the differential pairs, preserving signal integrity at Gigabit speeds. High-capacitance TVS diodes distort the signal and can cause return loss failures.
  • GDTs have very low capacitance (typically < 1 pF) and have negligible effect on signal integrity, making them ideal as the first line of protection at the connector entry.
